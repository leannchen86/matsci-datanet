ML for materials discovery · everything so far, on one page · 18 Sep 2026

Open lab data has no trusted labels and no fair exam.

That is the whole story of 15 chats and 10 reports. ImageNet changed vision because it paired labels people trusted with a hidden-label test with fixed rules. Measured (not simulated) materials data has neither yet, for four reasons. All four can be worked on from a laptop.

An “ImageNet moment”
ML progress on real lab data becomes measurable
=
Trusted labels
records you can train on without inheriting their mistakes
+
A fair exam
a test set and a marking rule that reward more than memorising

Why the labels are not trusted

1
Records contain mistakes that nothing catches
Label noise with no validator. Unit slips, sign flips, simulated files filed as measured.
2
Records do not say how the sample was made or measured
Missing features. Two samples with the same formula are different “images” of one class.

Why the exam is not fair

3
Test questions leak into training
Random splits test on formulas and papers the model has already seen, like splitting by scan instead of by patient.
4
Some tasks have no answer key, and no written marking rule
Ground truth is one annotator’s opinion, and the grader’s string matching decides the score.

Volume is not the missing piece. Simulated databases hold over a million materials. Open records that pair a recipe with its raw X-ray scan number about 1,400 (1,035 + 352).

What we actually found

own experiments on public files · one number per claim
1
Records contain mistakes that nothing catches
646 of 13,702
thermoelectric records fail their own checksum by more than 50%. The check is possible on 13,702 of 55,422 samples. 266 of the failures are clean power-of-ten slips.
The efficiency score zT is computed from three other measured curves, so you can recompute it and compare. A free flag, not a fix. Another database already ships a similar filter.
1,702 of 3,019
“RAW” powder files in the RRUFF mineral database are calculated, not measured. Their own headers say so.
Synthetic data sitting in the “real” folder. Not news: the headers declare it. In the PROCESSED files your project uses, the share is 198 of 1,484.
414 of 499
All 499 patterns in one folder of opXRD (a pooled open collection of X-ray files from several labs) have values identical to RRUFF files. 414 of them are calculated, while their “is simulated” field reads False.
New: the opXRD paper does not name RRUFF. These files carry no answer label, so the harm is a contaminated “measured” pool, not inflated scores. Usable after cleaning: 1,683 of the 7,183 files on disk. That total counts some RRUFF samples twice and holds only 2,680 of opXRD’s 92,552 files.
15 of 361
shared comparisons between two databases that read the same published plots differ by more than 10%. Typical disagreement is only 0.5 to 0.9%.
Reading numbers off plots is not the problem. Those 15 look like record errors, judged without opening the papers. Scope: 102 sample pairs from one database’s high-scoring papers.
2
Records do not say how the sample was made or measured
32.7%
median difference in the Seebeck coefficient (voltage per degree) when two papers report the same chemical formula.
The formula is the class name; each physical sample is a different image. A published multi-lab test on one shared sample found only about 6% spread for this property.
15.0%
error floor for any model that sees only the formula, even one that already knows every answer. Measured on the 3,593 of 12,222 rows whose formula appears in more than one paper.
Irreducible error from missing features. On those rows a plain lookup table (22.9%) beats a 5-nearest-neighbour model (29.4%).
0 of 115 fields
in the largest open recipe-plus-scan release record how a sample was stored or handled.
Re-scans more than 180 days apart changed their fitted answer in 105 of 136 cases (77%), against 12 of 25 (48%) within 30 days. Exploratory: ageing and analysis drift cannot be told apart.
71 of 3,019
RRUFF powder files (2.4%) state the X-ray wavelength used.
Like audio files without a sample rate. Across the whole usable pool (RRUFF plus opXRD, 1,683 files) only 353 state it, and 352 of those come from one institution.

the recommendation · after adversarial checking

Start with a one-to-two-day test, not a build: is the X-ray marking problem real, or a bug in one marker?

One 40-row table answers it. (“Marking” is the scoring rule that decides whether a program’s answer counts as correct: the metric.) The answer decides whether the “fair marking kit” is a tool other people need, or a fix and a test file for your own project. Both outcomes are useful and both are cheap.

  • It is the smallest step that decides the most. The famous swing from 12 to 32 of 40 turned out to have three causes, and only one is a marking rule. Nobody has counted what is left once formula spelling is cleaned up (the same compound written as a different string, such as “Ni1.875O2” for NiO).
  • It has a sure first user: you. A pilot probe beside your project scores 0.309 under strict marking and 0.745 under lenient marking (n = 55).
  • No download, no model, no lab, no message to anyone. The 40 scans and stored results already sit in your own project folders. It needs two yeses from you: a folder name for the rescue copy, and read-only access to those folders.
