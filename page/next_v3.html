the recommendation · after adversarial checking

Start with a one-day test, not a build: is the X-ray marking problem real, or a bug in one marker?

One 40-row table answers it. The answer decides whether the “fair marking kit” is a tool other people need, or a fix and a test file for your own project. Both outcomes are useful and both are cheap.

  • It is the smallest step that decides the most. The famous swing from 12 to 32 of 40 turned out to have three causes, and only one is a marking rule. Nobody has counted what is left once formula spelling is cleaned up.
  • It has a sure first user: you. Your project’s pilot numbers move from 0.309 to 0.745 (55 scans) with the marking rule alone.
  • No download, no model, no lab, no message to anyone. The 40 scans and stored results already sit in your own project folders.
Headline so farWhat we saidWhat the checkers found
X-ray marking“The marking rule moves the score from 12 to 32 of 40.”Three causes: the rule (12 vs 32), out-of-date stored marks (17 vs 32), a different reference library (32 vs 38). On a second set of 20 scans the rule moves scores by only 1–3 of 20, while the tools differ by 11–13.
Thermoelectric leak“Hold out whole papers and the error nearly doubles, 25.5% → 42.2%. Models memorise papers.”Mostly “new formulas are hard”: 48.1% error on the 8,629 test rows whose formula was never seen. Where the formula was seen (3,593 rows), holding out the paper costs only 24.8% → 29.4%.
Simulated X-ray files“Calculated files filed as measured inflate test scores.”The 499 matching files carry no answer label, so they cannot inflate a score. The harm is a contaminated “measured” pool. Smaller, still real.

What survives, and ties every thread together: in each set-up we measured, how the exam is set and marked moved the score at least as much as the contestants differed (X-ray: 38 vs 35 of 40 is a tie; thermoelectrics: a lookup table at 22.9% beats the model at 29.4%; a 134-item company test has ±4.3 points of noise against a 2-point lead). It is a pattern in our own measurements, not yet a field-wide fact.
